**Unit-level caveat on the premise itself, from a source this unit already holds.** This catalog is
organised around the *interaction format* — what the learner is asked to produce, and in what response
space. One of its own sources says outright that the format is not where the leverage sits. U1-S16,
Discussion: "During recent years it has been shown that not the answer format itself, but the stimulus
set by the question (e.g. integration of the question into a medical context) influences the results."

this file is evidence that changing the format alone changes what a learner learns or what an
instrument measures; on the one head-to-head comparison held here (U1-S16) it did not. The case, hand
or scenario the format is wrapped around may matter more than the format. **Evidence-backed**, with
its own limit stated: a single source, in medical education, reporting an established finding it cites
rather than a result of its own.

### 1. Debrief ordering — the tail **confirms** self-first and materially strengthens it

The unit currently says §6.3 recommends a self-first debrief with a "may" verb while §7.3 and §7.6 state no order,
and concludes "Nothing in the guide *requires* the learner to go first, and that narrow claim stands." **That
narrow claim survives — no "shall" appears anywhere — but the characterisation of §6.3 as an isolated "may" is
understated. The tail states the sequence three more times, with stronger verbs, and devotes an entire section to
arguing for it.** All verbatim:

- **§16.2.8 "Debriefing and Critique" (folio 16-3, PDF 63):** "Facilitators should provide both positive and
  negative feedback during critiques of individual and crew performance. Prior to the facilitator's critiques,
  crewmembers should be encouraged to critique themselves." The verb is "should be encouraged", not "may".
- **§17.6 "Self Critique versus Facilitator Critique" (folio 17-4, PDF 68)** — a whole section on this question,
  and the strongest statement in the document: "Experience has shown that crews frequently debrief themselves.
  Self-criticism and self-examination are almost always present in these situations and in many cases they are
  much more effective than facilitator criticism. Frequently crews are more critical of themselves than the
  facilitator would ever be. Thus, the facilitator should do everything possible to foster this sort of
  self-analysis while at the same time keeping it at a constructive level." The same section adds: "However,
  unless absolutely necessary, the facilitator should avoid "lectures" about what is right and what is wrong."
- **§17.8 "Summary" (folio 17-5, PDF 69):** "In summary, the effective LOS facilitator will lead the crewmembers
  through self-critique of their performance."
- **§17.2.1 (folio 17-2, PDF 66)** and **§17.4 (folio 17-3, PDF 67)** both carry the same briefing promise: "The
  opportunity for full self-analysis is provided during the debriefing. The LOS facilitator will take notes only
  to assist in this debriefing." and "There will be an opportunity for full self-analysis during the debriefing."

**What cuts the other way, recorded rather than smoothed:** the guide is not univocal. **§17.7 (folio 17-5)**
states "During debriefing both total crew performance and individual performances should be openly discussed and
assessed by the facilitator." And the suggested debriefing format on the same folio 17-5 opens with the
*facilitator*, not the learner: "A positive general statement should open the discussion." followed by
"Crewmembers should then be encouraged to discuss the operation both as a whole and in parts." and closing with
"At the appropriate time, the facilitator should summarize and recap the key learning points in the debriefing."
So the guide's one *procedural* format is facilitator-open → crew-discuss → facilitator-close, while its
*principled* statements (§6.3, §16.2.8, §17.6, §17.8) all put the crew's self-critique before the facilitator's
critique. **Net effect on U1-5: its repaired position is correct and now rests on four loci rather than one, but
the sentence "§7.3 and §7.6 … simply do not repeat the ordering" should no longer be read as implying the guide
is near-silent on it. It is not. It returns to the question repeatedly and lands on self-first.**

### 2. Interruption — the tail **contradicts** the unit's scoping of the exception

U1-5 currently states the no-correction rule "carries a stated exception" and that "the uninterrupted run is
unqualified only for **Recurrent** LOFT", the exception being §7.5/§8.2's Qualification-LOFT carve-out. **The tail
holds at least two further in-run facilitator-intervention licences that are *not* scoped to Qualification LOFT.
They are triggered by learner state — overload, negative learning — not by LOFT variant.**

- **Folio 18-2/18-3 (PDF 72–73), chapter 18's Step 1:** "The LOFT facilitator should not routinely add to or
  modify a scripted situation, but, if the facilitator observes that a crew is overloaded to such an extent that
  further learning is impossible, he should be permitted to exercise *reasonable* judgment to prevent further
  compounding of the crew's situation. This can be done either by deleting planned problems, or through
  assistance rendered within the context of the scenario. For example, ATC might become more "helpful," company
  maintenance might propose a solution to a systems problem, or the dispatcher might be able to provide some
  useful assistance via a "SELCAL" message to the trip." This says "The LOFT facilitator", unqualified. Note the
  mechanism is **in-fiction** — the help arrives through a scenario role, so the run is never broken. That is a
  materially different device from stopping the scenario, and it is the one a trainer could copy.
- **Folio 17-2 (PDF 66), §17.2.1:** "The facilitator's role in LOS is to manage the training situation to
  maximize learning. This does not include scenario interruptions to "teach" right solutions, or to "test" the
  trainees. It does include facilitator guidance to prevent scenario degradation to negative learning and
  reinforcement of preferred or standardized solutions to problems." This reuses §7.5's "negative learning"
  trigger but **drops the Qualification-LOFT restriction** and applies to LOS generally.
- **Folio 18-7 (PDF 77), chapter 18's Step 8:** when a crew departs the script, "the LOFT coordinator has the
  option of permitting the selected action and supporting it with appropriate clearances, and weather, or
  alternatively, preventing the selected action by providing adverse weather, closed airports, or inoperative
  navigational aids. The latter 'course should be utilized with care since in many cases it is preferable to
  allow crews to proceed as they elect." [sic, stray apostrophe before "course"]
- **A third format permits interruption outright, and the unit has not catalogued it:** §9.2 (folio 9-1), "SPOT
  permits direct instruction and allows for interruption of the scenario by the facilitator." SPOT is not LOFT —
  §9.1, "It may not be substituted for recurrent line-operational flight training (LOFT) or qualification LOFT."
  — and is now catalogued as **U1-8**.

**What confirms the unit, enumerated positively so the balance is on the record.** The tail also restates the
no-interruption rule four times: §16.2.5 (folio 16-3) "The facilitator should remain as unobtrusive as possible
within the physical limitations of the simulator. You should resist the temptation to instruct, and must not
intrude in any way into the situation."; §17.4 (folio 17-4) "As emphasized in Chapter 7, in order to achieve the
highest degree of perceived realism, it is imperative that the facilitator neither intervene nor intrude in any
way into a LOFT scenario."; folio 18-3 "The use of certain simulator capabilities to provide replay, to be
frozen, to be repositioned, etc., which are not consistent with a continuous, real-time operation should not be
permitted within the LOFT context"; and §11.2 (folio 11-1) extending "must run uninterrupted" to the LOE.
**Net effect on U1-5: the rule holds, but the exception clause is incomplete as written. "One exception the guide
states for Qualification LOFT" is not accurate — there are at least three intervention licences, and two of them
are not variant-scoped.**

### 3. Grading, scoring and jeopardy — the tail **contradicts** the unit twice

U1-5's "Measures poorly" cell states flatly: "It produces no score at all." Table 3 records §6.3's "LOFT should
not be used as a method of checking the performance of individuals."

- **Folio 21-1 (PDF 104) — the decisive contradiction.** Appendix 1 is a suggested assessment form for a LINE
  ORIENTED SIMULATION session with a NAME field, sixteen graded rows and a four-point scale printed
  "1.Not Yet Competent | 2.Below Standard | 3. Satisfactory | 4.Very Good", closing over a PILOT SIGNATURE box.
  Full transcription is in the Table 2 row. **A per-individual numeric grade sheet is not "no score at all."**
  *Scoping caveat, stated because it matters and because overclaiming here would be the same defect this pass
  exists to fix:* the form's header band reads "INITIAL SIMULATOR TRAINING", so it is plausibly attached to
  Initial LOFT — which the guide treats as a distinct application at §12.2.1 — rather than to Recurrent LOFT,
  the variant U1-5 is scoped to. The form itself does not say which. What it does say is "LINE ORIENTED
  SIMULATION", and it is captioned "Example of the above Scenario's suggested Assessment Form", the "above
  Scenario" being chapter 18's LOS examples.
- **§17.1 (folio 17-1, PDF 65) — a direct denial of the no-jeopardy premise.** "Furthermore, everyone involved in
  any training program is charged with a responsibility for the continuing safety of flight operations through
  ensuring that the people in the system meet acceptable proficiency and performance standards. For these
  reasons, there is no such thing as a "no-Jeopardy" training exercise. In any training program, at some point a
  decision is made that the trainee has progressed satisfactorily through the program; otherwise, additional
  training is provided." [sic, source capitalisation "no-Jeopardy"] The chapter opens by naming this as a
  tension in itself — "In some ways, there is an apparent conflict inherent in the discussion that follows." —
  and resolves it not by denying assessment but by demanding a safe atmosphere: "Yet it is essential to create
  an atmosphere in which mistakes and errors can be made without fear of failure, embarrassment or punishment."
  **So the guide asserts §7.4's "LOFT is 'no-jeopardy' training" and §17.1's "there is no such thing as a
  'no-Jeopardy' training exercise" in the same document.** U1-5 quotes only the first.
- **§17.7 "Satisfactory Completion" (folio 17-5)** makes the same point operationally: "While "satisfactory
  completion" is an inescapable aspect of LOFT, at the same time it is hard to imagine "unsatisfactory training"
  if conducted appropriately." … "However, in some cases, mistakes may indicate deficiencies that do require
  additional work." … "Critical assessment of an individual can be mentioned in the presence of the full crew,
  but remedial details should be handled privately. Tact is required to avoid the appearance of checking rather
  than training." That last clause concedes individual critical assessment occurs and must be *managed for
  appearance* — a weaker position than "not used to check individuals".
- **§13.5/§13.5.1 (folios 13-6/13-7)** supply a named rubric for assessing crew CRM *in a LOFT scenario*; **§11.3
  (folio 11-1)** gives the LOE grading method ("grade an aircrew's observed skill at accomplishing technical and
  CRM criteria of success using a grading scale"); **§12.3 (folio 12-4)** records "LOS has been utilized by at
  least one operator to conduct proficiency checks."
- **What still supports the unit:** the anti-jeopardy machinery in the tail is real. Folio 13-3, "Bulk erasure of
  taped sessions is suggested to encourage candour among participants while assuring their privacy."; §16.2.7
  (folio 16-3), "Following review of the videotape, the tape may be erased."; §17.1, the fear-of-failure sentence
  above. **Net effect on U1-5: "It produces no score at all" is contradicted by the guide's own appendix and
  should be withdrawn or narrowed. The defensible version is that the *facilitator issues no pass/fail grade
  during a Recurrent LOFT debrief* (§7.4), not that the format yields no score.**
