# U3 — Non-vendor literature on interactive exercise and assessment formats

Unit question: what does non-vendor literature say about interactive exercise and assessment
formats, and about what each format does and does not measure? This unit is the register's
independence counterweight: every source below is peer-reviewed academic literature or a
federally-funded research-centre report, opened first-hand in full text on 2026-07-26.

| U3-1 | Unaided free-recall production ("write down everything") | Roediger, Agarwal, McDaniel & McDermott (2011), *JEP: Applied* 17(4) 382-395, Experiment 1 | U3-S2 | Given only a topic label and a blank sheet, writes out everything they can recall of the material; scored by number of idea units recalled. Not an MCQ: nothing is presented to select from; the entire response is produced. | Discriminates at a low, uncompressed point on the scale where recognition formats are at ceiling. Exp 1 Table 2 means (p.386, **Mean** column): free recall .30 (SD .10) tested vs .20 (SD .15) nontested, F(1,35) = 63.22, partial eta-sq = .64; the same students' multiple-choice chapter-exam means were .94 vs .81, i.e. near ceiling. | Content-sensitive and unstable across material. The free-recall testing effect appeared on three of the four chapters only, and the authors state (p.385) "We have no ready explanation for why the testing effect differed across four chapters of material and did not occur for the chapter on ancient China." Absolute scores are low (.30/.20), so a single session yields a coarse signal. | U3-2 | Short-answer production test with a lesson-wide word bank | Roediger, Agarwal, McDaniel & McDermott (2011), Experiment 3 | U3-S2 | Writes the answer to a question in their own words; a list of all key terms from the whole lesson is available as an aid, but no per-item option set is offered. | Not a dealt hand: the prompt is a content question, not a position with legal actions. Not an MCQ: the word bank spans the entire lesson rather than being a per-item option set, so the interaction is not "select one of a presented finite option set" — the answer text must be produced. Flagged as the most borderline row in this table on test (b). | Sits lower on the measurement scale than the matched multiple-choice form and still detects the practice effect. Table 5 means: short answer .84 (SD .13) tested vs .75 (SD .17) nontested, F(1,65) = 52.44, partial eta-sq = .45; matched multiple-choice .90 vs .82. Authors' stated motive (p.388) was that short answer "may permit us to examine performance at a lower point on the measurement scale". | Format cost is real and the benefit did not survive delay. p.390, in the section headed "Chapter exam performance: Short answer": "Performance was generally poorer for the short answer test by 6-7% because of the requirement to produce rather than recognize correct answers" — so scores are not comparable across formats. | U3-3 | Guidance-faded worked example / completion problem | Kalyuga, Ayres, Chandler & Sweller (2003), *Educational Psychologist* 38(1) 23-31 | U3-S1 | Works through a partially completed solution in which some steps are shown and others are blank, supplying the omitted steps; the proportion of omitted steps increases as expertise grows. | Not a dealt hand: the presented artefact is a worked solution with omissions and the learner's input is a missing intermediate reasoning step, not a terminal action choice on one position state. Not an MCQ: nothing is offered to select from; the step must be supplied. | Whether the learner can carry the specific reasoning step currently being faded, at a controlled level of support. The fading schedule is itself a running measure of where support is still required. p.27: "detailed worked examples were most appropriate when presented to novices, but that they should be gradually faded out with increased levels of learner knowledge and be replaced by problems"; a fading-out procedure "was superior to an abrupt switch from worked examples to problems". | A score obtained under guidance is confounded with the learner's expertise level, so guided items do not order learners by knowledge. The effect is defined at p.24 as an instructional design that is "beneficial for novices ... but disadvantageous for more expert learners", i.e. the same faded item reverses in sign across expertise. p.29: "Without such tailoring, the effectiveness of instructional designs is likely to be random. Indeed, recommendations to use particular designs can be potentially quite counterproductive." This is a narrative review of prior experiments across varied domains, not a single controlled trial; it reports no pooled effect size. | Seed B's fading-assistance ladder for chart literacy: classify hard/soft/pair, then locate the dealer column, then read the cell, with steps progressively withheld. | Evidence-backed | independent / Product judgement |
| U3-4 | Contrasting-cases invention task (invent an index or rule that discriminates the cases) | Schwartz & Martin (2004), *Cognition and Instruction* 22(2) 129-184 | U3-S7 | Sees several deliberately contrasted small cases side by side and writes a single general procedure that yields a comparable value or decision for all of them, before being told the canonical method. Figure 1 prompt, p.135: "Your task is to invent a procedure for computing a reliability index for each of the pitching machines. There is no single way to do this, but you have to use the same procedure for each machine, so it is a fair comparison between the machines." | Not a dealt hand: several contrasted cases are presented at once and the required output is a general procedure, not an action on one position. Not an MCQ: the output is a free-form written procedure with no option set. | Whether the learner notices the features that distinguish the cases, and their readiness to learn from a subsequent explanation. Exp 2 Table 4, total acceptable solutions on the target transfer problem: invention plus embedded test resource 45.8% (n = 23) vs tell-and-practice plus resource 21.9% (n = 31); invention without resource 18.2% (n = 20) vs tell-and-practice without resource 26.6% (n = 28). Three-way interaction F(1,98) = 4.4, MSE = 0.30. | Scoring the invented answer for correctness is a bad signal — the answers are usually wrong. p.130: "students typically generate suboptimal solutions, and therefore do poorly on subsequent assessments of problem solving". p.161: "the students in the inventing condition did not generate a correct standardizing procedure during instruction, yet they were more prepared to learn the procedure". Benefits were narrow: p.166 "the benefits of production were relatively topic specific", and "Perhaps a longer intervention would lead to a more general preparation for learning, but that evidence is not forthcoming in this research". Two design experiments, ninth-grade statistics, N = 102 in Exp 2. The authors' own generalisation caveat, p.169, which this row previously omitted: "These studies used relatively small sample sizes and narrow demographics"; classes were assigned to treatments intact, so "we cannot claim that the results generalize to other classes without a larger sample of classrooms", and although that is mitigated by the within-class resource contrast and by the Experiment 2 replication, "Nevertheless, the use of intact classrooms to implement the experimental design limits the generalization of the claims". | Seed A's ruleset-delta view: show baseline and changed tables side by side and ask the learner to write the rule for when the change moves the answer, before showing the delta cells. | Evidence-backed | independent / Product judgement |
| U3-5 | Dynamic assessment with an embedded learning resource (double-transfer test) | Schwartz & Martin (2004), assessment experiment | U3-S7 | During the test itself, meets a worked-example resource inside one item and must spontaneously apply it to a later target item; nobody signals the connection. | Not a dealt hand: what is measured is whether the learner picks up and carries a resource across two test items, not an action choice on a presented position. Not an MCQ: no option set at either item. | Preparation for future learning — whether prior activity left the learner able to learn from a resource in the moment. p.161: "dynamic assessments can be sensitive to levels of understanding that we care about but that can be missed by summative assessments of problem solving. The two forms of instruction would have looked the same had we not included the resource item from which students could learn." | Easily confounded by surface context matching. In Experiment 1 the authors flag exactly this (p.162): the invention advantage "possible that the advantage of the invention condition had nothing to do with the method of instruction, but instead, derived from using a sports context that matched the sports context of the resource problem"; Experiment 2 was run to remove that confound. Cell sizes are small (n = 20 to 31, Exp 2 Table 4) and even the best cell reaches only 45.8% acceptable. p.167 notes 33.8% of students showed negative transfer on a related item. The p.169 generalisation caveat applies to this experiment too — "For the assessment experiment, we assigned classes to instructional treatments ... the use of intact classrooms to implement the experimental design limits the generalization of the claims". | Whether a learner drilled on H17 can pick up an S17 rule card presented mid-session and apply it unprompted — the Subject C "ruleset transfer" target. | Evidence-backed | independent / Product judgement |
| U3-6 | Construct-a-concept-map with learner-created linking phrases | Yin, Vanides, Ruiz-Primo, Ayala & Shavelson (2004), CSE Report 624, CRESST / Stanford | U3-S4 | Given a fixed set of domain concepts on movable tokens, arranges them on a blank page, draws one-way arrows between pairs, and labels each arrow with a relationship phrase of their own wording, forming propositions. | Not a dealt hand: no position state and no action set; the output is a labelled directed graph. Not an MCQ: with nine supplied concepts the report notes 36 undirected proposition permutations are available and the labels are free text, so the interaction is construction, not selection of one option. | Partial knowledge and structural richness. Created-phrase (C) maps put 40.9% of propositions in the midrange 1-2 band vs 15.1% for the selected-phrase (S) variant (Table 4); C produced 10.2 propositions on average vs 7.6 for S, t(92) = 3.85, p < .01; complex network structures appeared in 55.1% of C maps vs 26.7% of S maps, chi-sq(1, N = 94) = 7.81, p < .05. Abstract: "The C mapping technique is better than S in capturing students' partial knowledge". | Weak reliability and, in this study, weak criterion validity — this row is a direct counterweight to any blanket claim that freer construction measures better. Interrater reliability on C propositions reached 0.81 (vs 0.92 for S) only after detailed rules were written; p.11: "The interrater reliability for the scoring of this database was initially quite low." Correlation with the teacher's achievement ranking was only .243 and non-significant for C vs .551 (p < .05) for the more constrained S, though "the two correlations do not differ significantly, z = 1.44, p > 0.05" (p.15). The two formats are not interchangeable: pooled stability .766 (n = 49) vs pooled equivalence .460 (n = 45), z = 2.39, p < .01. Mean total accuracy was 15.48 against an expert map score of 30. N = 94 eighth-graders, one buoyancy unit. | Whether the learner holds "dealer weak on 2-6 → let the dealer bust → stand on stiffs" as a connected causal structure or as isolated memorised cells — the `how-to-teach.md` Part 4 claim that bust rates must be taught before the chart. | Evidence-backed | independent / Product judgement |
| U3-7 | Fragment-assembly ordering task (Parsons-style) | Ericson, Denny, Prather, Duran, Hellas, Leinonen, Miller, Morrison, Pearce & Rodger (2022), ITiCSE-WGR '22 working group report | U3-S5 | Given a scrambled set of solution fragments, some of which may be distractors that must be left unused, drags them into a correct order to form a complete solution. Section 2.3: "Parsons problems are tasks where the user needs to construct a solution by placing fragments into a correct order." | Not a dealt hand: no position state with a legal-action set; the learner assembles an ordered sequence. Not an MCQ: the response is a permutation and subset over the fragment pool, not selection of one option from a presented set. | Sequencing and procedural-structure knowledge at reduced generation load. Section 1 (Introduction), p.2 col.1 — not the Section 2 intro: Parsons problems "greatly constrain the problem-solving space while at the same time expose students to syntax and common code structures and patterns". Nine of the reviewed papers present cognitive-load evidence (Section 4.4.6), and Table 10 shows learning-gain evidence in 22 papers and speed evidence in 11. The review's own closing verdicts on those two categories are positive, and belong here alongside the negatives quoted opposite: "Overall, evidence in this category is high quality" (4.4.1, learning gains, p.22) and "The evidence that Parsons problems can impact student cognitive load is extremely high quality" (4.4.6, p.24). | It is explicitly a scaffolded proxy, not the authentic production task — the very next sentence, same paragraph on p.2 col.1, calls them "a simpler, scaffolded alternative to more authentic code writing tasks" (the two quotes are adjacent sentences, not one). The review is blunt about evidence quality: of 11 code-writing papers, "only one study explicitly listed clear threats to validity and only three used more than one group" (4.4.4); for the 7 code-tracing papers, limitations were "explicitly discussed in its own part in 2 papers" (4.4.7); for learning gains, "only half of the studies had clearly detailed threats to validity listed" (4.4.1); and overall "Most types of evidence presented still require large-scale replications to verify their findings" (4.4). Section 4.4.14 notes three papers finding "students can struggle with Parsons problems if not properly equipped to tackle them". Domain is computing education throughout. These negatives are the review's own — and so are the two positive quality verdicts quoted in *Measures well*; this cell used to carry only the negatives, and both halves are the same reviewers' judgement. | Order of play and hand resolution; the classify → dealer-column → cell lookup sequence; the dealer's fixed procedure as an ordered routine rather than a remembered outcome. | Evidence-backed | independent / Product judgement |
| U3-8 | Continuous probability estimate scored by a proper scoring rule | Mellers, Ungar, Baron, Ramos, Gurcay, Fincher, Scott, Moore, Atanasov, Swift, Murray, Stone & Tetlock (2014), *Psychological Science* 25(5) | U3-S6 | Enters a probability for a stated uncertain outcome and may revise it as often as desired before it resolves. p.4: "They predicted the chance the event would occur, on a scale from 0% (certain it will not occur) to 100% (certain it will occur)." | Not a dealt hand: the input is a number about an uncertain event, not an action for a presented position. Not an MCQ: the response space is continuous, so there is no finite presented option set. | Calibration and resolution as separable components, and overconfidence directly. p.3: the Brier score "measures individual accuracy; 0 is the best score, and 2 is the worst score ... extreme, incorrect forecasts are heavily punished". p.6: "all points in Figures 2 and 3 fall below the identity lines, which means that forecasters were overconfident; overconfidence was worse among forecasters without training than among those with training." Training improved Year 1 Brier scores, F(2,1586) = 14.29, p < .001 (p.4) — but that omnibus F tests training overall across all three conditions (no training, scenario training, probability training), not the probability module. The probability-module contrast is narrower and much weaker: "Probability training was more effective than scenario training, t(1053) = 2.23, p = .026" (p.4). Scenario and probability training each "took approximately 45 min" (p.3); in Year 2 probability training beat no training, F(1,882) = 19.12, p < .001. | A raw score confounds item difficulty with skill, and single items are near-useless. p.6: "Because variability bears on question difficulty rather than skill, we do not discuss it further." Because participants chose their own questions the authors "removed selection effects by standardizing scores within questions" (Results, p.4). On one close-call question, trained teams averaged 1.44 — worse than untrained independent forecasters at 1.31 (p.9). Sample was highly selected: a bachelor's degree was required, 83% male, 64% with postgraduate training, mean age 36 (p.3). The Year 2 no-training condition contained previously scenario-trained participants, which the authors say "tipped the scale against finding a statistically significant effect of training" (p.3). | Dealer bust-rate and EV estimation, and the variance literacy that `how-to-teach.md` Part 0 makes the product's differentiator: does the learner's stated confidence track reality, independent of whether the hand won. | Evidence-backed | independent / Product judgement |
| U3-9 | Convergent-cue generation (one produced answer fits a set of cues) | Kornell & Bjork (2008), *Psychological Science* 19(6) 585-592, supplementary word-generation experiment | U3-S3 | Sees a set of several cues that share one common answer and types that single answer. p.590: participants figure out "the single word that could be used to fill in the blanks in each of 12 sets of six words". | Not a dealt hand: the stimulus is a set of cues, with no position state and no legal-action set. Not an MCQ: the answer is typed with nothing presented to select. | Not something the source measures or claims. Kornell & Bjork's dependent measure in this experiment was later *memory* for the target word — "later memory for the target words was significantly better in the massed condition than in the spaced condition (.34 vs. .22)" (p.590) — plus whether the problem could be solved at all; abstraction is nowhere assessed. The reading that the format indexes whether the learner has abstracted the element common to superficially different instances, rather than memorising instance by instance, is this catalog's inference from the task structure (one produced answer must satisfy six unlike cues), which is why the row's evidence label is `Product judgement`. | Performance is dominated by the presentation schedule rather than by knowledge, and in a direction opposite to the rest of this literature. Here spacing "made it nearly impossible to solve the problems, and, thus, later memory for the target words was significantly better in the massed condition than in the spaced condition (.34 vs. .22), t(19) = 2.78, p < .05, prep = .94, d = 0.65". The authors concede at p.591: "Admittedly, this simple experiment was contrived to be a situation in which massing, not spacing, would enhance the generation and memory of the critical words." One experiment, N = 20, word stimuli, no replication reported. | "These five hands all take the same action — name it": abstraction of the stiff-versus-weak-dealer principle across cells rather than per-cell recall. | Product judgement | independent / Product judgement |

### 1. Rodriguez (2003) could not be opened, and nothing is claimed from it at first hand

The gap was named around Rodriguez (2003), *Journal of Educational Measurement* 40(2) 163-184.
Two prior verifiers surfaced it via search summaries and recorded its details as unverified;
**this pass did not open it either.** Enumerating positively what was attempted and what each
returned:

2. Unpaywall API for DOI 10.1111/j.1745-3984.2003.tb01102.x — `"is_oa":false`, `"oa_locations":[]`, `"oa_locations_embargoed":[]`.
3. Semantic Scholar Graph API for the same DOI — closed access, no `openAccessPdf`, abstract withheld by publisher.
4. ResearchGate record 229787988 — HTTP 403 Forbidden.
5. CORE API title search — HTTP 403 Forbidden.
6. Four web searches (including a `filetype:pdf` search and a search restricted to `eric.ed.gov` / `files.eric.ed.gov`) — no open full text; ERIC holds no record of this article.

Bibliographic metadata *was* confirmed first-hand from two documents actually opened (see U3-S12):
Rodriguez's own CV and U3-S11's reference list both give *J. Educ. Meas.* 40(2), 163-184. So the
citation is real and correctly stated. **Its findings remain unverified at first hand by anyone in
this register**, and none of the claims attributed to it in the gap statement — 56 disattenuated
correlations, a stem-equivalent correlation "near unity" — are repeated here as fact.

They are not, however, unreported. **U3-S11 summarises the whole prior meta-analytic literature on
this exact question at p.3, Rodriguez included, and that summary is the most decisive material this
register holds on the "near unity" claim.** Verbatim, p.3: "Traub [31] and Rodriguez [25] included
nine and 67 primary studies in their meta-analyses, respectively, and focused mainly on correlations
between scores from OE and CE response formats. Traub [31] concluded that the number of studies was
too small to validly assess construct equivalence. ... **Rodriguez [25] identified an uncorrected
pooled correlation coefficient of 0.67 and concluded that construct equivalence appeared to be at
least partly a function of item design (e.g. stem-equivalency; [25])**." The same page notes that
"Rodriguez [25] focused exclusively on correlations, entirely excluding studies that examined
difference hypotheses", and completes the field with Ryan & DeMark [32] (two meta-analyses on sex
differences, 14 and 23 primary studies) and In'nami & Koizumi [33] (37 primary studies), whose
results "indicated that CE formats were easier for test-takers than OE formats (Hedges' g = 0.65),
especially when between-subjects designs or stem-equivalent items were used". U3-S11 restates the
comparison against its own pooled estimate at p.16: "The uncorrected correlation coefficient of 0.67
revealed in our meta-analyses is as high as the relation reported by Rodriguez [25] in his previous
meta-analysis."

**What that settles, and what it does not.** Rodriguez's own headline pooled figure is **0.67** —
identical to U3-S11's independently derived pooled r, and nowhere near unity — so the gap statement's
"near unity" attribution is not what Rodriguez reported as his pooled correlation, and it is defeated
as an *uncorrected* claim by a source that read him. It does **not** adjudicate a *disattenuated*
(corrected-for-unreliability) figure, which could be materially higher and which no source in this
register reports; and it does not refute Rodriguez's own conclusion that construct equivalence is
partly a function of item design including stem-equivalency — a conclusion U3-S11's own
Meta-Analyses A supports at score level (§3(c) below). Evidence level for this paragraph:
**Evidence-backed at one remove** — it is U3-S11's first-hand report of Rodriguez, not Rodriguez.

The fallback named in the brief, Rodriguez (2005) in *Educational Measurement: Issues and Practice*
24(2), does **not** address this gap: Rodriguez's CV, p.6 publication 11, gives its title as "Three
options are optimal for multiple-choice items: A meta-analysis of 80 years of research", which is
about the number of options per item, not about constructed-response equivalence. It was therefore
not pursued.

### 2. What was read instead

U3-S11: Breuer, Scherndl & Ortner (2023), "Effects of response format on achievement and aptitude
assessment results: multi-level random effects meta-analyses", *R. Soc. Open Sci.* 10: 220456 —
open access (CC-BY), read in full as 25 rendered PDF pages, with the publisher's JATS XML used to
cross-check the table cells. It is a later, larger, open synthesis of the same question (102 primary
studies, 392 effect sizes) and it cites Rodriguez (2003) as its reference 25. Funding statement,
p.20: "We received no funding for this study." Competing interests, p.20: "We declare we have no
competing interests."

### 3. The boundary condition, in the source's own terms

**(a) The synthesis only ever covers construct-matched format pairs.** p.5 §2.1 states the
exclusion: "studies that did not present clearly separable effect sizes for the OE and CE parts of a
test ... and studies in which the OE and CE parts did not measure exactly the same construct". Every
number below is therefore about open-ended (OE) and closed-ended (CE) *versions of the same
assessment content*. It is silent about formats that are not construct-matched to any multiple-choice
item — which is U3-3 through U3-9. **U3-1 does not belong in that bucket.** Its free-recall exam and
the multiple-choice chapter exam were the first and second parts of the *same* chapter exam over the
*same* target facts (U3-S2 p.384: "The first part of the chapter exam consisted of a free recall exam
... Following the free recall exam, students completed a multiple-choice exam comprised of all tested
and nontested items"), so U3-1 is content-matched to a multiple-choice test though not
stem-equivalent to it. It therefore sits inside U3-S11's scope on content-match and outside it on
stem-equivalence — and its own numbers are exactly what Meta-Analyses A predicts for that case: a
large score-level gap (.30/.20 free recall against .94/.81 multiple choice on the same content) with
no rank-order correlation reported in either direction.

**(b) Across construct-matched pairs, the two formats rank learners very similarly.** Pooled
correlation between OE and CE scores, Meta-Analyses B: **r = 0.67** (s.e. 0.050), 95% CI [0.57,
0.76], p < 0.001, k = 98 effect sizes from 34 primary studies (Table 4, p.13). The authors' own
gloss, p.12: "This means that a large part (i.e. 41-72%) of the variance in performance on OE
assessments is explained by performance on CE assessments." These are **uncorrected** correlations,
not disattenuated (p.19: "most of the original studies included here did not report corrected
estimates or sufficient information to correct for potentially biased effects").

**(c) What stem-equivalence moves is score *level*, not rank order.** Meta-Analyses A (score
differences, negative = higher scores on CE) pooled d_av = -0.65, 95% CI [-0.78, -0.53], k = 303
(Table 2, p.10). Stem-equivalency significantly moderated it — Table 3, p.11, total row:
stem-equivalent k = 242, d = **-0.52**; non-stem-equivalent k = 61, d = **-0.88**; d_diff = -0.36,
p = 0.004; and for domain-specific knowledge k = 106, d = -0.54 vs k = 26, d = -1.03, d_diff = -0.49,
p = 0.017. Text, pp.9-10: "Overall, analyses revealed a significantly smaller difference between
scores from OE and CE formats for stem-equivalent items than for non-stem-equivalent items
(d_diff = -0.36, p = 0.004), as hypothesized." Implication drawn by the authors, p.19: "performance
test scores were most similar when stem-equivalent items were used throughout the different formats.
This indicates that differences in scores may arise primarily from different content being assessed
with different formats."

**(d) The operative bound for *this* catalog is OE format type, not stem-equivalence.** Table 5,
p.14, total row for type of OE response format: *written short answer* k = 52, **r = 0.81** versus
*other OE* k = 46, **r = 0.51**; r_diff = -0.30, p < 0.001 — the only significant moderator of the
correlation in that table. Text, p.13: "A significantly stronger association between scores from OE
and CE assessments was revealed when written short answer items (k = 52; r = 0.80, p < 0.001), which
are most similar to typical CE items, were used rather than essay items (k = 16; r = 0.46, p < 0.001;
r_diff = -0.34, p < 0.001)." (The *comparison* figures differ because the table row aggregates all
non-short-answer OE types — k = 46, r = 0.51 — while the sentence contrasts short answer against
essay specifically — k = 16, r = 0.46; both are reported as printed. Aggregation does not, however,
explain the other movement: the **written-short-answer estimate itself shifts from r = 0.81 in
Table 5 to r = 0.80 in the p.13 sentence at an unchanged k = 52**. The shift is immaterial to the
boundary statement — 0.80 and 0.81 support the same conclusion — but it is reported rather than
smoothed, and the higher, table-printed 0.81 is the one carried above.)

**(e) U3-S11's third meta-analysis runs against the direction this catalog leans, and is carried
anyway.** Meta-Analyses C (91 primary studies, 337 effect sizes; §3.3, pp.13-16) asks how each format
relates to *external* criteria rather than to the other format. On criterion validity against school
performance, the closed-ended format wins. Table 8, p.16, total row: k = 17, r_OE = **0.27**,
r_CE = **0.38**, r_diff = 0.11, p = 0.002; and for domain-specific knowledge k = 12, r_OE = 0.28,
r_CE = 0.42, r_diff = 0.14, p = 0.001. Text, p.16: "In an exploratory moderator analysis, we found
that response format significantly moderated the relation between test scores and school performance.
Overall, significantly larger correlation coefficients were revealed when CE items (r = 0.38,
p < 0.001) were used compared with OE items (r = 0.27, p < 0.001; r_diff = 0.11, p = 0.002;
table 8)." Discussion restatement, p.18: "Finally, analyses revealed significantly higher criterion
validity scores for academic success for achievement and aptitude tests with CE response formats
compared with OE formats."

**Honesty constraints that travel with (e), all from the same source.** The analysis is labelled
**exploratory** by the authors themselves ("In an exploratory moderator analysis", p.16). It rests on
**k = 17** effect sizes. And two results in the same paper run the other way. First, sex differences
are *larger* in CE formats: Table 7, p.15, total k = 183, d_OE = 0.06 versus d_CE = 0.12,
d_diff = 0.06, p < 0.001 in favour of men, with a significant effect *favouring women* on OE
assessments in reading and writing (d_diff = 0.15, p < 0.001) — a fairness cost of CE, not a
measurement gain. Second, achievement motivation relates more strongly to OE performance: Table 8,
p.16, k = **5**, r_OE = 0.39 versus r_CE = 0.17, r_diff = -0.22, p = 0.031, which the authors read at
p.17 as indicating "that giving one's best effort may be particularly performance-enhancing when
responses have to be constructed". That k = 5 sits **below the paper's own stated caution threshold**
— p.19: "our results concerning individual characteristics (e.g. test anxiety, risk propensity and
achievement motivation) were based on a small sample of studies that included fewer than 10 effect
sizes each, further research is required here." The school-performance result at k = 17 is above that
threshold and the motivation counterweight is below it, so the counterweight is the weaker of the two
and is recorded as such rather than used to cancel the finding.

**Direction check against this catalog.** (e) runs the *same* way as U3-6, the one Table 1 row that
already carries a counter-directional result: there the freest constructed format correlated .243
(non-significant) with the teacher's achievement ranking while the more constrained selected-phrase
variant reached .551. Two unrelated literatures, one small classroom study and one meta-analysis over
91 studies, point the same way on external criteria. That is inconvenient for the premise that freer
production formats measure the real thing better, and it is written here for exactly that reason.

**So the boundary statement this register should carry is:** *where a constructed-response item is
matched to a multiple-choice item on the same content — and especially where the constructed response
is a short written answer — the two formats correlate strongly enough that "the MCQ measures this
poorly and the produced answer measures it well" is not supported as a claim about who knows more.
What differs reliably in that case is the score level, not the ordering of learners. The catalog's
`Measures poorly` cells for multiple choice hold as stated only for comparisons that are not
content-matched to a multiple-choice item.* Of the nine patterns in Table 1, **U3-2 is the one most
exposed by this**: its own source (U3-S2) reports the short-answer and matched multiple-choice forms
moving together, and U3-S11 places short-answer at the high-correlation end (r = 0.81).

Item (e) adds a second limit that is not about construct equivalence at all, and that reaches rows
the first limit does not: on the one *external* criterion U3-S11 could test, the closed-ended format
was the **better** predictor. So "the produced answer measures the real thing better than the
multiple-choice item does" cannot be assumed even where the two are not content-matched. Nothing in
Table 1 claims otherwise on external criteria, and no row's `Measures poorly` cell is withdrawn by
this — but any future step from "measures something the MCQ does not" to "measures it better" needs
its own evidence, and this register now holds a meta-analytic result pointing the other way.

### 4. Where U3-S11 runs *against* the expectation that prompted this pass

Reported as found, because it is inconvenient in both directions.

- **Stem-equivalence did not significantly moderate the correlation.** Table 5, p.14, stem-equivalency
  total row: stem-equivalent (category "yes") k = 86, r = **0.62**; non-stem-equivalent (category "no")
  k = 12, r = **0.85**; r_diff = 0.23, **p = 0.064**. As printed, the numerically *higher* correlation
  is in the non-stem-equivalent subgroup and rests on only 12 effect sizes — though which subgroup
  carries which label is itself in doubt for Meta-Analyses B (see §5); the non-significance is not.
  Text, pp.12-13: stem-equivalency
  "did not significantly moderate the relation between scores from OE and CE assessments, indicating a
  large association between scores from OE and CE response formats irrespective of stem-equivalency."
  The clean "stem-equivalent → correlation approaches unity" story the gap statement anticipated is
  **not** what this source's correlation data show.
- **Score-level agreement and rank-order agreement are decoupled.** p.12: "The Pearson's
  product-moment correlation between 39 effects that were available as both standardized mean
  differences and as correlation coefficients revealed a small non-significant relation of -0.03. This
  indicates that larger correlation coefficients between scores from OE and CE assessments did not
  coincide with smaller standardized mean differences between scores from the two formats."
- **The authors' own conclusion is not "the formats are equivalent".** p.20: "Even though strong
  relations were revealed between the results of the two most popular response formats used in
  achievement and aptitude testing (i.e. OE and CE), the medium-sized to strong negative pooled effect
  sizes identified for the differences between scores from these two response formats strengthen the
  doubts about their construct equivalence postulated earlier".

### 5. Caveats carried from the source, and one internal inconsistency

- Heterogeneity is extreme: I²_total = 99.17 for the pooled correlation (Q(97) = 7776.83, p < 0.001;
  Table 4, p.13) and 99.83 for the pooled difference (Table 2, p.10). Pooled means here summarise a
  very dispersed literature.
- Funnel asymmetry was detected; the authors attribute it to heterogeneity rather than publication
  bias, and state (p.19) "the reported effect sizes within this study are probably underestimations and
  are likely to represent the lower bounds of validity".
- Risk of bias: p.19, "most primary studies did not include random sampling, random allocation of
  participants to the formats, randomization of the items, blinding or attrition management with regard
  to the research question about response formats".
- Domain is achievement and aptitude testing in schools and universities — over 100 different tests,
  clustered by the Cattell-Horn-Carroll model (p.6). This is one synthesis over one literature; it is
  not evidence about a procedural-skill trainer.
- **An inconsistency inside U3-S11, now stated as arithmetic rather than as a units mismatch.** The
  descriptive paragraphs say most authors used "non-stem-equivalent items (61.7%)" for Meta-Analyses A
  (p.9) and "(79.4%)" for Meta-Analyses B (p.12), yet Tables 3 and 5 assign the large majority of
  *effect sizes* to the stem-equivalent category (242 of 303; 86 of 98). **For Meta-Analyses A this is
  only a lopsided units mismatch, and it is arithmetically possible.** A draws on 81 primary studies
  and 303 effect sizes (p.9); 61.7% of 81 is 50 studies non-stem-equivalent, leaving 31, and 50
  studies can produce Table 3's 61 non-stem-equivalent effect sizes (1.2 each) while 31 produce 242
  (7.8 each) — unbalanced, but not impossible. **For Meta-Analyses B it is not possible.** B draws on
  34 primary studies and 98 effect sizes (p.12); 79.4% of 34 is exactly 27 studies non-stem-equivalent,
  leaving 7 stem-equivalent. Table 5 puts **12** effect sizes in the non-stem-equivalent category and
  **86** in the stem-equivalent one. Every primary study contributes at least one effect size, so 27
  studies cannot yield 12. The narrative percentage and the table's category assignment cannot both be
  correct for B: one of the two has the labels the wrong way round. The paper does not reconcile them
  and this pass did not resolve it.
- **Why §4's conclusion survives that anyway.** Swapping the two labels in Table 5 exchanges which
  subgroup carries r = 0.62 (k = 86) and which carries r = 0.85 (k = 12); it leaves r_diff = 0.23 and
  p = 0.064 untouched. The moderator is non-significant under **either** labelling, so what §4 rests
  on — that stem-equivalence does not significantly moderate the OE/CE correlation, and that no "near
  unity under stem-equivalence" story appears in this source's correlation data — holds either way.
  What does *not* survive intact is the weaker directional remark that the numerically higher
  correlation sits in the non-stem-equivalent subgroup: that reading depends on the labels in doubt,
  and §4 now says so. Meta-Analyses A is in better shape: its table direction is independently
  corroborated by the narrative on p.10 ("test-takers achieved even higher scores on CE tests compared
  with OE exams when non-stem-equivalent items were used"), which matches category "no" carrying the
  larger d of -0.88. Meta-Analyses B has no equivalent independent corroboration.
